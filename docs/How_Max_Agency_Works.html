1 · The 30-second version

The whole idea in one breath Max Agency is a team of AI robots that act like a small software company. You hand them a goal ("write me a book", "build me an app"). One robot writes a plan, another checks the plan, a "manager" robot chops the plan into tasks and pins them to a shared to-do board, and worker robots pick up tasks and do them. Every finished piece of work is checked by a different robot before you give the final yes. The robots never sleep — they wake up every minute, look at the board, and move things forward.

🧠 They plan

An Architect robot turns your goal into a written plan. A CTO robot reviews it like a strict teacher.

🔨 They build

A manager robot hands out tasks. Worker robots write the actual files and propose changes.

✅ You approve

Nothing important happens without a human. Only you press the final "merge" button.

Why this is coolYou describe what you want once. The robots handle the how — splitting work, doing it, checking each other, and asking you only when they truly need a decision.

2 · The big idea

Imagine a tiny company where every employee is an AI, and instead of meetings they use sticky notes.

Analogy: the kitchen brigade Think of a restaurant kitchen. The head chef writes the menu (the plan). A food critic tastes a sample and says yes/no (the review). The kitchen manager calls out orders and pins tickets to the rail. Cooks grab tickets, cook the dish, and put it on the pass. Nothing leaves the kitchen until it's checked. Max Agency is that kitchen — but the "dishes" are pieces of a software or writing project, and the "tickets" are tasks on a website called GitHub.

The two things that make it work

1. A shared board everyone can see

All the robots read and write to the same online to-do board (GitHub Issues). Because the board is the single source of truth, a robot can "forget everything", wake up, read the board, and know exactly what to do next. No robot has to remember anything in its head.

2. Colored labels that act like traffic lights

Each task wears small colored labels. Labels say who should do it, what kind of job it is, and what stage it's at. Robots only grab tasks whose labels match their job — like a cook only taking tickets for their station.

ExampleA task labelled ready + assigned:hermes-coder + role:coder means: "This is ready to start, and the Hermes worker-robot should do it." Any other robot ignores it.

4 · The three places things live

One of the most confusing things at first: there are three separate copies of things. Here's the map.

The #1 beginner mistakeMixing up the engine (the toolkit that powers the robots) with the workshop (the actual project being built). They are different GitHub repos. Never copy files between them. The engine is the same for every project; you get a fresh workshop for each new project.

Robots can't chat in a hallway. Instead they leave notes on a shared board — GitHub Issues — and read the colored labels on each note.

6 · The life of a task

This is the heart of everything. Follow one task from "just an idea" to "done and approved."

1Born

The Architect's PLAN.md is sliced into tasks. Each becomes a board note tagged backlog (waiting) or ready (good to go), plus its model + role + phase labels.

2Unlocked

A task often waits on others ("Depends-on: #5"). The Orchestrator watches; when the things it needs are closed, it flips backlog → ready.

3Handed out

The Orchestrator dispatches a ready task → in-progress and leaves a note: "dispatched to Hermes."

4Built

The matching coder claims it (puts its name on it), makes a fresh branch, writes the files, and opens a pull request (a proposed change) that says "Closes #N." The task flips to review.

5Checked

The Orchestrator opens a CTO review note. The CTO reads the change + the automatic tests (CI), then writes VERDICT: APPROVED or CHANGES REQUIRED.

6Approved & merged

On APPROVED, the Orchestrator pings you to merge. You click merge; the Orchestrator closes the task. 🎉 If CHANGES REQUIRED, it bounces back to step 4.

What "CI" meansCI = a robot that automatically tries to build the whole project every time something changes. If the build breaks, CI turns red and the CTO will refuse to approve. If it builds fine, CI is green. Green CI is a hard requirement to merge.

8 · The two engines

The worker robots actually run on two different computers-within-your-computer. They never share memory — they only meet on the GitHub board.

Two separate worlds, one shared board. That's why everything important is written on GitHub — it's the only place they both can see.
Why "stateless" mattersBecause each robot wakes up fresh with no memory, the board has to hold the truth. This is actually a superpower: if a robot crashes, the next one just reads the board and carries on. Nothing is lost.

9 · When things go wrong (the safety nets)

Real systems hit snags. Max Agency has automatic catches so a single hiccup doesn't freeze everything.

🔴 The build breaks (red CI)

A coder's change won't build. The CTO sees red CI and writes CHANGES REQUIRED. The task bounces back to in-progress and a coder tries again. Nothing broken ever reaches you.

💤 A worker dies mid-job

If a robot grabs a task then crashes, the task could be stuck with its name on it forever. The Orchestrator notices "claimed, but no work after a while," un-claims it, and lets a fresh worker take over.

❓ A genuine judgment call

If the CTO hits something only a human should decide, it writes VERDICT: ESCALATE. The Orchestrator forwards it to you (e.g. by Telegram) and pauses that thread.

🚦 Out of fuel (rate limits)

AI models have usage caps. If a robot hits its limit, its ticks fail quietly and simply retry later once the limit resets. The board waits patiently.

AnalogyThink of these like the safety features in a car: the seatbelt (human-only merge), the reversing camera (the CTO check), and the engine warning light (escalations). They don't drive the car for you — they stop small problems becoming crashes.

10 · Glossary

Every bit of jargon in this guide, in plain words.

WordPlain meaning
Repo (repository)A project folder stored on GitHub, with full history of every change.
IssueA note on the shared board describing one task. The robots' to-do items.
LabelA small colored tag on an issue. Says who/what/which-stage.
BranchA private workspace copy where a coder makes changes without disturbing the main project.
Pull Request (PR)A proposal: "here are my changes, please review and merge them in."
MergeAccepting a pull request — folding the changes into the real project. Human-only.
CI (Continuous Integration)A robot that auto-builds/tests the project on every change. Green = good, red = broken.
PLAN.mdThe Architect's written blueprint: phases, tasks, and what depends on what.
TickOne "wake up, look at the board, do one thing, go back to sleep" cycle of a robot.
DispatchThe Orchestrator handing a ready task to a worker (moving it to in-progress).
VerdictThe CTO's decision on a piece of work: APPROVED, CHANGES REQUIRED, or ESCALATE.
Escalate"A human needs to decide this." The robots stop and ask you.

WSL"Windows Subsystem for Linux" — a Linux computer running inside your Windows PC. Home of Hermes.
HermesThe Linux-side engine running the Orchestrator and the bulk coder.
Claude CodeThe Windows-side engine that can act as Architect, CTO, or the quality coder.

11 · One-page cheat sheet

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 The five roles

  • Architect — writes the plan
  • CTO — reviews & approves
  • Orchestrator — hands out work, chases progress
  • Hermes Coder — cheap bulk builder (Linux)
  • Claude Coder — careful builder (Windows)

🚦 The stages

  • backlog → waiting on dependencies
  • ready → good to start
  • in-progress → a coder is on it
  • review → CTO checking
  • merged → closed → done ✓

🏷️ Reading a label set

in-progress + assigned:hermes-coder + role:coder + phase:1
= "Hermes should build this Phase-1 task; it's started."

👤 Only you do these three

  • Approve the plan
  • Merge finished pull requests
  • Answer escalations

🗺️ The three places

  • Engine — the robot toolkit (Max_Agency)
  • Copy — Hermes's auto-mirror of it
  • Workshop — your actual project repo

🛟 If it stalls

  • Red CI → coder retries automatically
  • Stuck task → Orchestrator reclaims it
  • Out of fuel → waits for the limit to reset
  • Big question → escalates to you
Remember this one sentence You give a goal; a plan gets written and checked; a manager robot hands out tasks; worker robots build them; a reviewer robot checks each one; and you press the final button. Everything else is just the plumbing that makes that loop run by itself, around the clock.
